📚 What Your Child Is Learning

🕸️ Word Web — Semantic Memory

Based on Spreading Activation Theory (Collins & Loftus, 1975). Teaches how concepts prime each other in memory. Kids learn: semantic networks, priming, schemas, retrieval practice, metacognition.

Curriculum Links: Psychology, Cognitive Science, Study Skills

⚡ Neuron Lab — Computational Neuroscience

Based on Izhikevich Neuron Model (2003). 6 biologically-plausible neuron types with real spiking dynamics. Kids learn: action potentials, E/I balance, neuron diversity, network dynamics.

Curriculum Links: Biology, Neuroscience, Physics, Computer Science

🗺️ Thought Paths — Graph Theory

Dijkstra's algorithm on cognitive architecture graphs. Kids learn: shortest paths, cognitive process flow, algorithmic thinking, brain connectivity.

Curriculum Links: Mathematics, Computer Science, Psychology

⚖️ Brain Power Meter — Cognitive Load Theory

Based on Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988). Intrinsic/Extraneous/Germane load, schema acquisition, automation. Kids learn: why learning feels hard, how to study smarter.

Curriculum Links: Study Skills, Educational Psychology, Executive Function

🛡️ Safety & Privacy

  • What is stored — one browser key named brainExplorerProgress holding XP and badge state. Nothing else.
  • What is never stored — no name, no age, no email, no photo, no location, no IP logging by us
  • Nothing leaves the device — the page makes no network requests at all after it loads
  • To erase it — clears with normal browser data clearing, or the Reset button in Progress
  • COPPA / GDPR-K — no personal information is collected from a child, so there is nothing to consent to

🔬 Real Scientists

The games you just played are the actual work of real people. Here are six of them, and what they figured out.

Danielle Bassett

Network neuroscientist

She treats the brain like the maps in Thought Paths: a set of regions joined by connections. Using graph maths, she showed that your brain's network actually re-wires itself while you learn something new.

Try this: in Word Web, activate one concept and count how many others light up. That number is what she measures for a whole brain.

Eve Marder

Neuroscientist, circuits

Her lab asked a simple question that turned out to be enormous: does a group of neurons behave the way the single neurons would predict? The answer is no. The same cells wired differently produce completely different behaviour.

Try this: in Neuron Lab, change how strongly the neurons are coupled without changing how many there are. The pattern changes anyway. That is her point.

Rita Levi-Montalcini

Neurobiologist, Nobel 1986

She discovered nerve growth factor — a chemical signal that tells growing neurons which way to reach. She did part of this work in a bedroom, in hiding, during the Second World War, with a microscope she built herself.

Try this: in Neuron Lab, notice that neurons only connect if a signal reaches them. Growth factor is one of those signals.

Brenda Milner

Neuropsychologist

She studied a patient known as H.M., who could not form new memories, and proved that memory is not one thing. You have several separate memory systems that can break independently — which is why Word Web and the Brain Power Meter are two different games.

Try this: in Word Web, ask why some words feel easier to reach than others. That feeling is one system talking to another.

Radia Perlman

Computer scientist, networks

She designed the spanning tree algorithm that stops a network from sending the same message round and round forever. Without it, connecting two networks would jam both of them.

Try this: in Thought Paths, find a route that visits no junction twice. You have just built a spanning tree.

Frances Allen

Computer scientist, compilers · Turing Award 2006

She worked out how to translate programs into faster machine instructions. Every smooth, instant animation on these pages runs on ideas she pioneered — she made the invisible translation step fast.

Try this: everything you click here reacts in under a second. That speed is compiler work, not magic.

One more thing

None of these six was "naturally" good at this. Each one got stuck, checked something, and tried again — usually for years. Getting stuck is the job. Your brain builds connections by using them, which means the confusing part is the part where you are actually learning.
